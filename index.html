Proposition: “The world is winning the fight against climate change.”

FOR the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Standardized by measuring percentage change since 1970 (+1.5). GDP and CO2 use different units, so putting both on the same scale makes the gap easy to see. Displaying different units with two y-axes would have been hard to read, so we instead decided to convert values to percentages to make the visual clearer. Also, this helps comparing the final values for the two lines easier, since both values are percentages.
  • Started from the year 1970, not 1960 (-1.5). The data we had access to actually starts at 1960, but we purposefully started at 1970 since the gap between the two lines is greatest when starting at this year than for other years. While this helps make the gap between the lines greater, a skeptical viewer might question why 1970 specifically is chosen, but this is not much of a problem since we made sure to choose a year dividable by 10, otherwise the starting year would seem too arbitrary.
  • Used emissions per person, not total (-1.5). Total emissions are up about 314% since 1960, which hurts our side. Per person looks much better, so we used that. One downside to this is that a clever viewer might realize how population has skyrocketed since 1970, but hopefully using GDP per capita as the other value might make the viewer focus more on per person change rather than absolute change.
  • Slanted title: "Growth Has Broken Away From Pollution" (-1). "Broken away" sounds permanent and causal. The chart only shows two trends moving apart. A neutral title would have been more honest and less persuasive, so we made sure to describe the graph in a way which highlights the growing gap between the two lines.
  • Teal for emissions, direct labels, y-axis from -10 to 120 (-0.5). Teal quietly says "good," and the tighter axis stretches the gap. The +105% and +17% labels give the takeaway with no legend, which we felt would make the visual too crowded. We also added a source note and said the figures are population weighted, which makes the chart look more credible.

AGAINST the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Plotted total emissions, not rates (+1). Total CO2 is the main drive for warming, so using CO2 as the metric is fair. We summed CO2 across individual countries only and also dropped the World and regional aggregate rows, so nothing was double counted. It's also the metric that makes the "winning" claim look weakest.
  • Y-axis goes from 0 to 25 Gt (-0.5). Using a tight axis which barely contains the highest value makes the graph very steep and looks like a big impact. Using a tight y-axis is not as noticable since we are not cutting anything off, so it will help amplify our point without seeming very deceptive.
  • Title and label: "No Peak in Sight" and "still climbing" (-1.5). The line somewhat flattened between 2013 and 2016 before rising again. These phrases ignore that plateau and make a bold prediction the data does not necessarily prove. While a careful viewer might notice how the line is starting to get flatter, the overall trend is still that the line is increasing, so most viewers will probably not notice how our phrasing might be inaccurate.
  • Red line and shaded area (-0.5). Red is generally read as alarm or something negative. The fill adds visual weight to the growth, since it has a large area, which grows very quickly, creating a sense of danger in the viewer's mind.
  • 1960 baseline and a big +314% label (+1). Choosing a farther back year, such as 1960, makes the percentage change much higher and therefore more impactful. The large label drives the number home. While a closer inspection would show that the percentage change is much less when starting from a year in the last few decade, the line is still increasing throughout that entire period, so most viewers will likely not think about this.

Final Reflection

Picking what to plot was the easier part, however, having to make each chart persuasive without being too obvious was much harder. Once the metric was chosen, everything else such as the title, color, and axis followed from it. The main goal was to not make anything too loud or else the readers would stop finding the graph trustworthy.

The main surprising factor was how much the denominator changes the story considering both charts use the same data. When looking at it through each individual, it seems that the world is improving. However, when looking at the same data in total, the world is actually getting worse.

Both charts use real information, and still the reader who saw the "For Proposition" would be leaving with a completely different perspective than the reader who saw the opposite one. A chart can be misleading without a single false number, it just needs to answer the right question. For example, in our visualizations, the winning chart can't show that total emissions rose 314% and the losing chart isn't showing that the economy grew a lot faster than emissions per person. Something that was also noticed is readers don't typically check axis ranges or baselines, so the deception also benefits the story without lying.

We would define ethical analysis and visualization as showing the data fairly, being open to other choices, and not relying on deception or anything that would leave the viewer with false ideas in their mind. Some good boundaries between what's considered acceptable versus misleading in this sense would be whether a reader is able to see the information you're sharing and is able to access the same data. Being private about something that would be important for the reader to know or distorting the information would be misleading. This includes cutting off the axis that the reader wouldn't notice or making a title that claims something bigger than the data. The tight y-axes and the "no peak in sight" label are fairly close to the borders. If this were a real setting, we would have to add a note to both charts.
